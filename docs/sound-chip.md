# The Game Boy Sound Chip & T3d Tunes

T3d Tunes turns the Game Boy's sound hardware into a playable looping instrument. To use it
well — and to understand *why* it behaves the way it does — it helps to know how the chip
itself works. This document is two halves:

1. **[How Game Boy audio works](#part-1--how-game-boy-audio-works)** — a thorough primer on
   the APU: the four channels, duty cycles, envelopes, sweep, the wave RAM, the noise LFSR,
   length counters, and the frame sequencer.
2. **[How T3d Tunes maps the chip onto the looper](#part-2--how-t3d-tunes-maps-the-chip)** —
   how we detach the chip, sample a ROM's sounds, and drive a 16-step loop.

---

## Part 1 — How Game Boy audio works

The Game Boy's audio is produced by its **APU** (Audio Processing Unit), a programmable
sound generator with **four channels**. There are no samples and no synthesis in the modern
sense — each channel is a tiny fixed-function tone generator you configure by writing bytes
to hardware registers in the range **`$FF10`–`$FF3F`**. The CPU (the game's music driver)
rewrites those registers many times a second to play a song.

All four channels are mixed, panned left/right, scaled by a master volume, and emitted at
the console's output. The chip runs off the 4.194304 MHz system clock.

### The four channels

| # | Name | What it makes | Defining feature |
|---|------|---------------|------------------|
| 1 | **Pulse 1** | A square wave | Duty cycle + **volume envelope** + **frequency sweep** |
| 2 | **Pulse 2** | A square wave | Duty cycle + volume envelope (no sweep) |
| 3 | **Wave** | An arbitrary 32-step waveform | A user-defined **wavetable** in RAM |
| 4 | **Noise** | Pseudo-random noise | A **linear-feedback shift register** (LFSR) |

T3d Tunes uses these as its four **lanes**: PUL1, PUL2, WAVE, NOIS.

### Pulse channels 1 & 2 (square waves)

A pulse channel outputs a square wave that's either "high" or "low". Three things shape it:

**Duty cycle** — what fraction of each period is "high". The hardware offers four duties,
each an 8-step pattern (this is exactly the table the emulator uses):

| Duty | Pattern | Sounds like |
|------|---------|-------------|
| 0 | `00000001` (12.5%) | thin, nasal |
| 1 | `10000001` (25%) | classic chiptune lead |
| 2 | `10000111` (50%) | full, hollow square |
| 3 | `01111110` (75%) | like 25% inverted |

**Frequency** — set by an 11-bit value *x* (0–2047) written across two registers. The pitch
in hertz is:

```
frequency_Hz = 131072 / (2048 − x)
```

So bigger *x* = higher pitch. T3d Tunes inverts this to turn a musical note into *x* (see
Part 2).

**Volume envelope** — the channel's volume (0–15) can automatically ramp up or down over
time. You set an **initial volume**, a **direction** (increase/decrease), and a **period**
(how many envelope ticks between each ±1 step). A decaying envelope is the classic
plucked-chiptune sound; period 0 disables the envelope so the note holds at a fixed volume.

**Sweep (channel 1 only)** — channel 1 can also slide its *frequency* up or down over time
(period, direction, shift amount). This is what makes coin-pickups and laser "pew" sounds.

### Channel 3 — the Wave channel

The wave channel is the interesting one: instead of a fixed square, it plays back a
**32-sample, 4-bit waveform** that you write into **Wave RAM** (`$FF30`–`$FF3F` — 16 bytes,
two 4-bit samples per byte). The channel marches through those 32 nibbles at a rate set by
the same 11-bit frequency value, looping. So whatever shape you put in Wave RAM *is* the
timbre — a sine, a saw, an organ, a vowel, anything.

Its volume isn't a 0–15 envelope; it's a coarse **volume code**: 100%, 50%, 25%, or muted
(the samples are shifted right by 0, 1, 2 bits, or silenced). It has no envelope, so on its
own it sustains until stopped — relevant later.

This is why **each ROM's wave channel is its own fingerprint**: every game ships its own set
of wavetables, and those are some of the most distinctive sounds T3d Tunes harvests.

### Channel 4 — the Noise channel

Noise is generated by a **linear-feedback shift register (LFSR)** — a shift register whose
feedback bit is the XOR of its low two bits, producing a pseudo-random stream of 1s and 0s
that sounds like static. Three controls shape it:

- **Clock shift** and **divisor** — together set how *fast* the LFSR is clocked, i.e. the
  noise's pitch/colour (from a low rumble to a hissy "tss").
- **Width mode** — 15-bit (full, hissy) or 7-bit (shorter loop → a more "tonal", buzzy
  noise).
- A **volume envelope**, same as the pulse channels.

Noise is what games use for percussion — kicks, snares, hats, explosions.

### The shared machinery

**The frame sequencer.** A single 512 Hz timer drives the time-based behaviours of every
channel, on a repeating 8-step cycle:

| Runs at | Clocks |
|---------|--------|
| 256 Hz | **Length counters** |
| 128 Hz | **Frequency sweep** (channel 1) |
| 64 Hz | **Volume envelopes** |

**Length counters.** Each channel has a length counter that, *if enabled*, counts down and
**switches the channel off** when it reaches zero. This is how the hardware plays a note of a
fixed duration without the CPU having to manually stop it. Pulse/noise lengths are up to
64 ticks (≈ 0.25 s) and the wave channel up to 256 ticks (≈ 1 s). T3d Tunes leans on this —
see "note gating" below.

**Triggering.** Writing a byte with the top bit set to a channel's high-frequency register
("trigger") (re)starts the note: it reloads the envelope, restarts the waveform, and turns
the channel on. This single bit is the "note on".

**The DAC and master controls.**
- Each channel has a **DAC** that must be powered (a non-zero volume/envelope, or the wave
  DAC bit) for any sound to leave it.
- **`NR50`** sets the master volume for the left and right outputs.
- **`NR51`** routes each of the four channels to the left and/or right speaker (panning).
- **`NR52`** is the master power switch; turning the APU off clears every channel's state.

### The register map (for reference)

| Register | Address | Channel | Holds |
|----------|---------|---------|-------|
| NR10 | `$FF10` | Pulse 1 | Sweep period / direction / shift |
| NR11 | `$FF11` | Pulse 1 | Duty + length load |
| NR12 | `$FF12` | Pulse 1 | Envelope (initial vol / dir / period) |
| NR13/14 | `$FF13/14` | Pulse 1 | Frequency low / high + trigger + length-enable |
| NR21–24 | `$FF16–19` | Pulse 2 | As above (no sweep) |
| NR30 | `$FF1A` | Wave | DAC power |
| NR31 | `$FF1B` | Wave | Length load |
| NR32 | `$FF1C` | Wave | Volume code |
| NR33/34 | `$FF1D/1E` | Wave | Frequency + trigger + length-enable |
| Wave RAM | `$FF30–3F` | Wave | The 32-sample waveform |
| NR41–44 | `$FF20–23` | Noise | Length / envelope / LFSR / trigger |
| NR50/51/52 | `$FF24–26` | — | Master volume / panning / power |

## Part 2 — How T3d Tunes maps the chip

T3d Tunes takes everything above and makes it playable. The core idea: **the APU is just a
register-driven tone generator, so we can run a second one with no game attached and write
its registers ourselves.**

### Detaching the chip

T3d Tunes creates a **standalone APU instance** with its own audio output — completely
separate from any running game. A sequencer clock steps it in real time and writes registers
to it. That's the whole trick: there's no "instrument synthesis" beyond driving the real
chip's registers, so everything sounds exactly like Game Boy hardware because it *is* the
same emulated hardware.

### Patches — a sound is a register recipe

A **patch** is just the set of register values that define one timbre:

- **Pulse:** duty + envelope (initial volume, direction, period).
- **Wave:** the 16-byte wavetable + volume code.
- **Noise:** envelope + the LFSR register (clock shift / width / divisor).

To "play a patch at a note", T3d Tunes writes those registers, writes the note's frequency,
and sets the trigger bit. Note → frequency uses the inverse of the hardware formula:

```
x = 2048 − 131072 / note_Hz      (clamped to 0…2047)
```

### Sampling a ROM's sounds

This is the headline feature. When you select a game (or open the drawer), T3d Tunes:

1. **Runs the ROM headlessly** for a few seconds — booting it and emulating it with no
   window, tapping Start so games that wait on a title screen begin their music.
2. **Taps every channel trigger.** Each time the game triggers a note, T3d Tunes reads back
   that channel's current register state — the exact duty/envelope, wavetable, or noise
   settings the game just used — and turns it into a patch.
3. **De-duplicates** identical recipes and keeps up to a dozen distinct sounds per channel.

The result is a palette of **that cartridge's own instruments** — its leads, its basses, its
custom wavetables, its drum-like noises — ready to play. (Nothing is recorded as audio; only
the register recipes are captured, so each sound is regenerated live by the chip.)

### The looper

Opening **T3d Tunes** from the bar at the bottom of the library expands it into a full-height
panel that fills the window. The top of the library — the console tabs, the sort menu and the
ROM list — stays visible above it, so you can keep browsing and **switch games without
leaving the instrument**. Top to bottom, the looper is:

- **Transport + FX row.** Play/Stop, a BPM control, **Clear**, and the synth FX knobs
  (see below).
- **Four lanes = the four channels.** Each lane has a **dropdown** of the sounds harvested
  for that channel — this is how you reach every captured timbre — plus a per-lane **Glide**
  switch, a **16-step grid**, a **pitch knob**, and a **mute** (the coloured dot). Toggling a
  step schedules that lane's sound to trigger on that step; the sequencer advances 16th-notes.
- **A playable keyboard** (C3–C5) with its own sound picker.
- **A tabbed feature area** — five panels (Rhythm, Timbre, Perform, Visual, ROM) spread the
  full width below the keyboard.
- **A live oscilloscope** in the top-right (it appears when the drawer opens, alongside the
  game's box art and details), drawing the instrument's output waveform.

**Pitch.** Pulse/wave lanes are transposed by the pitch knob (note → frequency as above).
The noise lane has no musical pitch, so its knob nudges the LFSR clock-shift to make the
noise brighter or darker. The knobs sweep the conventional way — lowest fully left, highest
fully right.

### The synth FX and groove

To the right of **Clear** sits a row of knobs and buttons that colour the whole instrument.
The five effects run as a real signal chain on the output (**filter → drive → delay →
reverb**), so they shape the loop *and* the keyboard:

| Control | What it does |
|---------|--------------|
| **Cutoff** | A resonant low-pass filter. Fully right is wide open; turn left to muffle. Log-mapped ~200 Hz → 20 kHz. |
| **Res** | Resonance — a volume peak right at the cutoff, for the "wah"/squelch as you sweep Cutoff. |
| **Drive** | Drive / bit-crush — grit and dirt, from a little warmth up to full crunch. |
| **Delay** | A tempo-synced echo (an eighth-note at the current BPM); the knob raises both mix and feedback. |
| **Reverb** | A hall reverb for space and tail. |
| **Swing** | Groove — delays the off-beat 16th-notes so the loop shuffles instead of sounding straight. |

A few more controls finish the row:

- **Glide** *(per lane)* — the switch on each pitched lane (PUL1, PUL2, WAVE) turns on
  **portamento**: instead of jumping, the pitch *slides* from the previous note to the next.
  Great for rubbery basslines and lead swoops. (The noise lane has no pitch, so no Glide.)
- **Reset** (↺) — snaps every FX knob, Swing, all Glide switches, and the per-lane pitch
  knobs back to their defaults, and flushes any delay/reverb tail.
- **Dice** (🎲) — randomises the pattern across all four lanes for instant inspiration.

### The keyboard

A **playable two-octave keyboard** (C3–C5) sits above the feature panels — a live soundboard
to perform over the loop and audition sounds:

- **Pick the sound** from the **Keyboard** dropdown. It lists the *entire* sampled library —
  every channel's captured sounds — each tagged with the channel it came from.
- **Play it** by clicking the keys, or straight from your computer keyboard. The keys are
  mapped left-to-right, chromatically, from the lowest key (C3): `Q W E R T Y U I O P`, then
  `A S D F G H J K L`, then `Z X C V B N` — so **all 25 keys have a shortcut**, and the bound
  letter is printed on each key. Typing is captured only while the drawer is open and you're
  not editing a text field.
- **Use FX** — a switch deciding whether the keyboard plays *through* the FX knobs above or
  **dry**. Off by default, so you hear the raw sampled sound; flip it on to play through your
  dialled-in filter, delay and reverb. (The running loop always honours the FX; this switch
  only changes the keyboard, and only while the sequencer is stopped.)

### The feature panels

Below the keyboard, a tab bar spans the full width and switches between five panels. **Rhythm**
and **Timbre** lay their controls out as **four channel cards** — one quarter each for PUL1,
PUL2, WAVE and NOIS — so every channel gets room for full-size knobs and controls.

- **Rhythm** — per-lane generative rhythm tools: **Length** (1–16, so lanes of different
  lengths drift in and out of phase — polymeter), **Direction** (forward / reverse / ping-pong
  / random), **Probability** (a % chance each step fires), and **Euclidean** fill (spread N
  hits evenly across the lane). A **Mutate** button nudges the whole pattern — flips a few
  steps to evolve it rather than reset it.
- **Timbre** — per-lane synthesis: an **Arpeggiator** (the lane rips through a chord shape —
  octave, major/minor triad, 5th, etc. — on one channel, the classic Game Boy "chord on a
  mono voice" trick), **PWM** (sweeps the pulse duty over the note), **Vibrato** (pitch
  wobble), and **Ratchets** (a step retriggers 1–8 times for rolls). The pitch-less noise lane
  gets ratchets only.
- **Perform** — global performance controls (see *Live looping* for the per-row recording):
  **Tap** tempo, a momentary **Stutter** pad, a **Pump** sidechain, and a **Clear All Loops**
  button that wipes every banked loop in one press.
- **Visual** — a draggable **wavetable editor** for the wave channel (draw the 32-sample
  table, or load Sine/Saw/Square presets) and **Export WAV** (records one loop of the live,
  FX'd output to a file).
- **ROM** — tools tied to the sampled cartridge: **Auto-compose** (builds a starter loop in
  the game's voice from its harvested sounds), per-lane **Sound-shuffle** (each hit grabs a
  random sampled patch for that channel), and **Mashup** (sample a *second* game and blend its
  sounds into the palette).

### Live looping

Live looping is built right into the sequencer rows. Every lane has its own **REC** button, so
you build a song the way you would on a looper — lay down one part, bank it, then layer the
next — lane by lane:

- **REC** — **banks** the lane's current grid pattern into a **loop layer**, then clears the
  editable grid. The banked loop keeps playing on the sequencer's clock, and the steps you
  banked show on the grid as small **dots**.
- **REC again** — lay a new pattern on the same lane and bank it on top to **stack another
  layer**. Steps banked more than once stack their dots (two banks → two dots), and the REC
  button shows the layer count.
- **⌥-click / right-click REC** — clears that lane's banked loop (the editable grid is left
  alone). The Perform tab's **Clear All Loops** wipes every lane's loop at once.

The crucial part: **the banked loop is independent of the editable grid.** The main **Clear**
wipes only the step grid and *keeps playing*, so your loops carry on looping — bank a part,
clear the grid underneath it, and build something new on top. (**Stop Sequencer** halts
everything.)

The **Perform** tab rounds this out with global controls: **Tap** tempo (tap a beat to set the
BPM), a momentary **Stutter** pad (hold to retrigger the current column for rolls/fills, at a
chosen rate), and **Pump** — a tempo-synced sidechain duck that makes the whole loop breathe.

### Note gating — why notes don't drone

On real hardware, a triggered channel sustains until its length counter expires, its
envelope decays to zero, or the game's music driver stops it. Detached from that driver, many
patches (especially wave patches, which have no envelope) would **ring forever**. So T3d
Tunes **enables the length counter on every trigger**, loaded for ~200 ms — making each note
a clean one-shot. This is the authentic hardware mechanism, just driven by us instead of the
game.

### Browse-to-swap

Because patches are cheap (just register values), changing the selected game while a loop is
playing **keeps the loop running and swaps in the new game's sounds** as soon as they're
sampled. You can audition the musical character of your whole library without stopping.

### Putting it together — two quick recipes

**Build a beat (step sequencing):**

1. Open **T3d Tunes** and pick a game — its sounds are sampled automatically.
2. For each lane, choose a sound from its dropdown (Pulse 1 for a lead, Pulse 2 for a bass,
   Wave for a pad/organ, Noise for drums).
3. Tap steps to build a pattern; set each lane's pitch with its knob, flip on **Glide** to
   slide, and reach for the **Rhythm** and **Timbre** panels for length/probability, arps,
   ratchets and more.
4. Dial in the **FX** — Cutoff/Res for filter sweeps, Drive for grit, Delay/Reverb for space,
   **Swing** for groove. **Dice** rolls a random pattern; **Reset** returns the FX to neutral.
5. Hit **Play**, and select a different game to hear your pattern in another cartridge's voice.

**Loop a song (live looping, on the sequencer rows):**

1. Hit **Play Sequencer** to start the clock and lay a pattern on a lane's grid.
2. Hit that lane's **REC** to bank it — the grid clears but the loop keeps playing (banked
   steps show as dots).
3. Lay a new pattern on the same lane and **REC** again to stack a layer; move to other lanes
   to build the rest of the arrangement.
4. Need a fresh canvas under your loops? Hit **Clear** — the grid empties but your banked
   loops keep playing. Drop in a **Stutter** fill or some **Pump** for groove.

That's the Game Boy sound chip — four little tone generators and a frame sequencer — turned
into a sampler-fed loop machine and live looper.
